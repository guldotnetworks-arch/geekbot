Adam. He is the first human being described in both the Bible and the Quran, and his story opens the history of humanity in both scriptures. The Bible describes God forming Adam from the dust of the ground and breathing life into his nostrils, making him a living being, as recorded in Genesis 2:7. The Quran describes God shaping Adam from clay and commanding the angels to prostrate before him as a sign of honor, as stated in Surah Al-Baqarah 2:30-33 and Surah Al-A'raf 7:11-12. In both accounts, one being refuses — called Satan in the Bible and Iblis in the Quran — and is expelled for his arrogance. Adam and his wife are placed in a garden and warned away from a specific tree. They are tempted and eat from it. Genesis 3:6 describes the woman being deceived by the serpent and eating the fruit, then giving some to her husband. The Quran, in Surah Al-A'raf 7:20-22, describes Iblis whispering to both of them together, and both eating. The Quran does not place the blame on the woman alone — it uses the dual form of the Arabic verb, addressing both equally. After their act, God addresses them, and they are sent to live on earth. In Genesis 3:17-19, this is framed as a curse. In the Quran, Surah Al-Baqarah 2:37, Adam receives words of repentance from God, God accepts them, and the event is treated as a forgiven lapse rather than an inherited sin. Both accounts establish the same arc: humanity begins with a divine trust, breaks it through temptation, and is sent into a world where the struggle between guidance and error will define all of history.

Cain and Abel. The Quran tells this story in Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:27-31, describing the two sons of Adam each offering a sacrifice to God. One offering was accepted and the other was not. The Quran does not specify the nature of the offerings, nor does it name the two sons. The Bible, in Genesis 4:3-5, names them Cain and Abel and specifies that Abel offered the firstborn of his flock while Cain offered the fruit of the ground — and that God favored Abel's offering. In both accounts, the rejected son turns violent. The Quran records him threatening to kill his brother, and the brother responding with calm dignity, saying he would not raise his hand even in self-defense and that the sin would fall entirely on the aggressor — Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:28. The killing takes place. Then both accounts show the killer not knowing what to do with the body. The Quran in 5:31 says God sent a raven that scratched the earth, and by watching it, the killer learned how to bury his brother — a detail absent from Genesis. The Quran then delivers one of its most often cited moral statements: that whoever kills a soul without justification, it is as though he has killed all of humanity, and whoever saves one, it is as though he has saved all of humanity — Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:32. Genesis 4:10-12 records God confronting the killer and telling him his brother's blood cries out from the ground, followed by a curse on the killer. Both accounts treat this as the first act of murder and use it to anchor a fundamental moral principle about the value of human life.

Noah. He is among the most frequently mentioned prophets in the Quran, appearing across dozens of verses, and the Quran dedicates an entire chapter — Surah Nuh, chapter 71 — exclusively to his story. The Biblical account in Genesis 6 through 9 and the Quranic account share the same foundation: God observes widespread corruption among humanity, Noah is chosen as a righteous man, he is commanded to build an ark and to carry believers and pairs of animals, a great flood covers the earth, and those on the ark are saved while others perish. The Quran in Surah Hud 11:36-37 records God telling Noah that none of his people will believe except those who already have, and commanding him to build the ark. In Genesis 6:14-16, God gives Noah specific construction instructions, including dimensions and materials. The Quran's most distinct addition is the fate of Noah's son. Surah Hud 11:42-43 records Noah calling to his son as the waves rose, pleading with him to come aboard. The son refuses, saying he will take shelter on a mountain. Noah's son drowns with the others. Noah then cries out to God, reminding Him of the promise to save his family. God responds in 11:46 that the son was not of his household because he did not act righteously, and that Noah should not ask about things he has no knowledge of. This moment — a father watching his son drown while calling to him — carries an emotional weight that the Biblical account, which focuses on the mechanics of the flood and the dimensions of the ark, does not develop. Both accounts end with the ark resting on elevated ground, the waters receding, and God making a covenant. Genesis 9:13 records God placing a rainbow in the sky as the sign of that covenant. The Quran does not mention the rainbow but records God's promise in Surah Hud 11:48.

Abraham. He is the central patriarch of all three Abrahamic faiths, and both the Bible and the Quran treat him as one of the most important figures in sacred history. Genesis 11 through 22 covers his life in detail. The Quran references him across numerous chapters, including Surah Al-Baqarah, Surah Ibrahim, Surah Al-Anbiya, Surah Al-Shu'ara, and Surah As-Saffat. Both scriptures agree that Abraham was called by God to leave his homeland, that he settled in a new land, that he had no children for much of his life, and that he was tested severely. The Quran adds material not found in Genesis — most notably, the story of Abraham's youth. Surah Al-Anbiya 21:51-58 describes a young Abraham already questioning the idols his people worshipped, asking them directly why they could not speak or respond, and eventually entering the temple and breaking all the idols except the largest, leaving the hammer around the statue's neck. When confronted, he mockingly suggested they ask the largest idol what happened. He was put on trial and condemned to be thrown into a fire. Surah Al-Anbiya 21:69 records God commanding the fire to be cool and safe, and Abraham emerging unharmed. The Bible does not include this story, though the destruction of idols is referenced later in Joshua 24:2 as part of Abraham's background. Both scriptures describe Abraham as a man of complete trust in God. The Quran in Surah An-Nisa 4:125 calls him the intimate friend of God — Khalilullah — a title that places him in a relationship of closeness with the divine that very few figures in any scripture are granted. Genesis 15:6 records that Abraham believed God and it was credited to him as righteousness, which the New Testament in Romans 4 uses as the cornerstone of the concept of faith-based justification.

The sacrifice of Abraham's son. Both the Bible and the Quran record a moment in which God commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son, and both record that God intervened at the last moment and provided an animal in the son's place. Genesis 22:1-13 is explicit that the son was Isaac. The Quran, in Surah As-Saffat 37:99-113, describes Abraham having a vision in which he was sacrificing his son, telling the son about it, and the son — responding with submission and courage — saying: do what you have been commanded, and you will find me, God willing, among the patient. The son in the Quran is not named in this passage. Surah As-Saffat 37:112 mentions the good news of Isaac being given after the event, which some scholars interpret as meaning Isaac was a later gift distinct from the son in the sacrifice, and that the unnamed son was Ishmael. Islamic scholarly tradition has largely held that the son was Ishmael, while Jewish and Christian tradition holds it was Isaac. The Quran's silence on the name in that passage is itself significant, and both traditions agree completely on what the story means: a test of total submission to God, fulfilled and honored. The festival of Eid al-Adha, observed by over a billion Muslims annually, commemorates this event, making it one of the most actively practiced stories shared between these two traditions.

Lot. He appears in both Genesis and the Quran as a nephew or companion of Abraham who settled in a prosperous city later destroyed for its people's transgressions. Genesis 13:12 places Lot in the cities of the plain. The Quran references Lot's story in Surah Al-A'raf 7:80-84, Surah Hud 11:77-83, and Surah Al-Hijr 15:57-77, among others. Both accounts describe angels arriving in the form of men, Lot receiving them as guests, the townspeople gathering outside demanding access to the guests, and Lot being unable to protect them. In Genesis 19:1-11, Lot tries to offer his daughters instead, a detail the Quran does not reproduce — the Quran in Surah Hud 11:78-80 simply records Lot lamenting that he had no power over the situation. The angels reveal their true nature, tell Lot to leave with his family, and warn him not to look back. Both accounts record his wife looking back and being destroyed. Genesis 19:26 says she became a pillar of salt. The Quran in Surah Hud 11:81 says she was among those who stayed behind and was struck. The city was then destroyed. The Quran in Surah Al-A'raf 7:84 says God rained upon them a rain — most commentators understand this to mean the same destruction described in Genesis as fire and brimstone raining from the sky. Both accounts use Lot's story to make a point about justice, the sanctity of the guest, and the consequences of widespread moral collapse.

Isaac. He is mentioned in the Quran in several places as a prophet and patriarch, including Surah Al-Baqarah 2:133, Surah Al-An'am 6:84, and Surah Maryam 19:49. The Bible in Genesis 17:19 records God telling Abraham that Sarah would bear him a son named Isaac and that God would establish His covenant with him. Genesis 21:1-7 records his birth, describing Sarah's laughter turning to joy. The Quran in Surah Hud 11:71 records the angels announcing the news of Isaac to Abraham's wife, who expressed amazement at the prospect of bearing a child in old age. Both scriptures confirm him as a chosen prophet and the father of Jacob, linking him to the entire prophetic lineage that follows. The Quran does not expand on his individual life beyond confirming his prophethood and his place in the chain from Abraham to Jacob to the later prophets. His role is one of continuity — carrying forward the covenant and the lineage through which the next generation of prophets would come.

Jacob. He is called Yaqub in the Quran and appears most significantly as the father of Joseph, though both scriptures acknowledge him as a prophet and patriarch in his own right. Genesis 28 through 35 covers his life, his dream of a ladder reaching heaven, his years working for Laban, and the renaming of Israel. The Quran focuses on Jacob primarily through his role in Surah Yusuf, chapter 12, where his character is drawn with patience and depth. When Joseph disappears, Jacob refuses to accept that his son is dead. Surah Yusuf 12:86 records him saying he complains of his grief and sorrow only to God and that he knows from God what others do not. Surah Yusuf 12:87 records him telling his sons never to despair of God's mercy, because none despair of it except those who disbelieve. Both accounts end with reunion — Genesis 46:29-30 describes Jacob weeping upon seeing Joseph alive in Egypt. The Quran in Surah Yusuf 12:96 records Joseph's shirt being sent to Jacob, and when it was placed on his face, his sight was restored. Jacob is presented in both scriptures as a man whose faith held through decades of grief — a portrait of patience that both traditions treat as exemplary.

Joseph. His story is told in Genesis 37 through 50 across fourteen chapters, and in the Quran in Surah Yusuf, chapter 12, which the Quran itself describes as the best of all stories — Surah Yusuf 12:3. The two accounts are remarkably aligned in their major points. Joseph was his father's favored son. He had a dream in Genesis 37:9 of the sun, moon, and eleven stars bowing to him. The Quran records the same dream in Surah Yusuf 12:4. His brothers grew jealous and threw him into a well. They brought his shirt stained with blood to his father. He was sold into slavery in Egypt and taken into the household of a prominent official — called Potiphar in Genesis 39:1, and referred to as Al-Aziz in the Quran. The official's wife tried to seduce him. He refused. She accused him falsely. He was imprisoned. In prison, he interpreted the dreams of two fellow prisoners. He eventually interpreted Pharaoh's dream of seven fat cows followed by seven lean ones as seven years of abundance followed by seven years of famine — Genesis 41:25-32 and Surah Yusuf 12:47-49. He rose to a high position in Egypt. When famine struck, his brothers came seeking grain and did not recognize him. He eventually revealed himself and forgave them. The Quran in Surah Yusuf 12:92 records Joseph saying: no blame will there be upon you today — God will forgive you. Genesis 50:20-21 records a nearly identical sentiment, with Joseph telling his brothers that what they intended for harm, God intended for good. The Quran develops the inner emotional life of Joseph, his temptation by the wife of Al-Aziz, and his prayer to God in prison — Surah Yusuf 12:33 — in ways that provide a fuller moral and spiritual texture than the Genesis account, though both agree completely on the shape and meaning of his life.

Job. His story is told at length in the Biblical book that bears his name and briefly in two Quranic passages — Surah Al-Anbiya 21:83-84 and Surah Sad 38:41-44. Job was a man of wealth, family, and faith who was struck by terrible suffering. The Biblical book of Job opens with a scene in which God draws attention to Job's righteousness and allows Satan to test him, stripping him of his children, his wealth, and his health in succession — Job 1:13-19 and 2:7. The Quran does not describe this opening dialogue. It begins with Job already in affliction, recording his prayer in Surah Al-Anbiya 21:83: harm has touched me, and You are the most merciful of those who show mercy. God responds in 21:84 by removing his distress and restoring his family. The Biblical account is far more elaborate, including the long dialogues with Job's three friends who argue that his suffering must be punishment for sin, and Job's refusal to accept that claim. God ultimately speaks to Job from the whirlwind in chapters 38 through 41, asking a series of unanswerable questions about creation that reduce Job to silence and humility. Job 42:10 records God restoring his fortunes and doubling them. Both scriptures use Job's story to anchor the same truth: that suffering is not always punishment, that patience under trial is a form of faith, and that God restores those who hold to Him without bitterness or despair.

Moses. No prophet is mentioned in the Quran more times than Moses, appearing in over thirty chapters. The Biblical account spans the books of Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. Both scriptures cover the same essential history. Pharaoh ordered the killing of Israelite male infants. Moses was placed in a basket on the river by his mother — Exodus 2:3 and Surah Al-Qasas 28:7. He was found and raised in Pharaoh's household. He killed an Egyptian who was beating an Israelite — Exodus 2:12 and Surah Al-Qasas 28:15. He fled to Midian, where he encountered and married into a local family, and spent years as a shepherd. He received his call from God at a burning bush — Exodus 3:2 and Surah Ta-Ha 20:9-16. God told him to return to Egypt and confront Pharaoh. Both accounts record Moses expressing doubt about his ability to speak convincingly — Exodus 4:10 and Surah Ta-Ha 20:25-28 — and both record God granting Aaron as a support. Moses performed miracles before Pharaoh — the staff becoming a serpent in Exodus 4:3 and Surah Al-A'raf 7:107. The plagues came upon Egypt. Pharaoh refused. The Quran confirms this sequence in Surah Al-A'raf 7:130-136 and Surah Al-Isra 17:101. The Israelites were led out of Egypt, pursued by Pharaoh's army, and reached the sea. Moses struck it with his staff and it parted. Exodus 14:21-22 records the waters dividing and the Israelites walking through on dry ground. Surah Ash-Shu'ara 26:63 records the same. Pharaoh's army followed and drowned. Exodus 14:28 and Surah Al-Baqarah 2:50 both confirm the army's destruction. Moses then received the Torah on the mountain. He was absent for forty days — Exodus 24:18 and Surah Al-A'raf 7:142. During that time his people made and worshipped a golden calf — Exodus 32:1-4 and Surah Ta-Ha 20:83-88. Both accounts record Moses' fury upon returning, his confrontation with Aaron, and his prayer for forgiveness on behalf of his people. The Quran adds a detail about Moses choosing seventy elders to witness God's response and the elders being struck by lightning and revived — Surah Al-A'raf 7:155. Both scriptures agree that the generation that refused to enter the promised land was made to wander for forty years — Numbers 14:33-34 and Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:26.

The body of Pharaoh. The Quran makes one statement about Pharaoh's death that has no direct parallel in the Biblical account. Surah Yunus 10:90-92 records Pharaoh declaring belief as he drowned, and God responding that his declaration came too late — but that God would preserve his body so that he could be a sign for those who come after. Exodus 14:28-30 records that not a single soldier of Pharaoh's army survived, but says nothing about the preservation of the body. The Quranic verse is notable because ancient Egypt's practice of mummification means many pharaonic bodies were in fact preserved. The verse does not claim to identify a specific mummy or pharaoh, and the historical identification remains a matter of ongoing scholarly debate with no settled conclusion. What the Quran offers is a theological point — that divine justice can extend beyond a person's death, turning the very body of a tyrant into a lesson for those who study history.

David. He is confirmed in both scriptures as a prophet, a king, and a man given a scripture. The Bible covers his life extensively from First Samuel 16 through First Kings 2. The Quran references him in Surah Al-Baqarah 2:251, Surah Al-Anbiya 21:78-80, Surah Saba 34:10-11, and Surah Sad 38:17-26. Both accounts describe a young David killing the giant warrior Goliath — called Jalut in the Quran — in a battle between the Israelites and their enemies. First Samuel 17:49-50 records David striking Goliath with a stone from a sling. Surah Al-Baqarah 2:251 records David killing Jalut, and God giving him kingship and wisdom. Both scriptures describe David as a man of praise — Psalm 8 and 23 are attributed to him in the Bible, and the Quran in Surah Al-Anbiya 21:79 records that the mountains and birds were caused to join David in glorifying God, and in Surah Saba 34:10, that iron became soft in his hands by God's permission, allowing him to craft coats of armor. The Quran in Surah Sad 38:21-25 tells a story of two men who came to David — one claiming the other had ninety-nine ewes and demanded his single ewe — and David ruling hastily in the claimant's favor before realizing he had not heard both sides. He fell prostrate and repented. This episode is widely understood by Muslim commentators as paralleling the circumstances involving Bathsheba in Second Samuel 11-12, though the Quran does not name Bathsheba or describe that story directly. The Biblical account in Second Samuel 12 records the prophet Nathan confronting David with a parable about a rich man who took a poor man's single lamb — a near-identical narrative device for the same moral lesson.

Solomon. He is given more extended narrative attention in the Quran than almost any other king, with his story spread across Surah Al-Anbiya 21:81-82, Surah An-Naml 27:15-44, Surah Saba 34:12-14, and Surah Sad 38:30-40. The Bible covers his life in First Kings 1 through 11 and Second Chronicles 1 through 9. Both scriptures agree that Solomon was David's son, that he was given extraordinary wisdom, that he ruled over a vast kingdom, that he built the great temple — referenced in the Bible in First Kings 6 — and that he had relationships with distant rulers. The Quran expands on elements the Bible does not describe. Surah An-Naml 27:16 records Solomon saying he was taught the speech of birds. A hoopoe bird brings him news of a queen in the south — the Queen of Sheba, identified in Arabic tradition as Bilqis — who rules a great kingdom and worships the sun. This parallels First Kings 10:1-2, which records the Queen of Sheba hearing of Solomon's fame and coming to test him with hard questions. The Quran's version in Surah An-Naml 27:22-44 is more detailed: Solomon sends her a letter, she sends gifts which he rejects, she arrives, and Solomon has her throne transported to his presence by a powerful figure from among those around him before she arrives — described in 27:40 as happening in the blink of an eye. She then enters a palace with a glass floor so clear she lifts her robe thinking it is water, before recognizing the truth and declaring her submission. First Kings 10:9 records the Queen of Sheba attributing Solomon's wisdom to God and blessing him, but does not describe her conversion. Surah Saba 34:12-13 describes the jinn building for Solomon what he willed — palaces, statues, and great basins. First Kings 5:13-16 records Solomon conscripting forced labor for his building projects, which some commentators read as paralleling the Quranic description of the jinn workforce. The Quran in Surah Saba 34:14 describes Solomon's death — he died leaning on his staff, and the jinn continued working, thinking he was alive, until a worm ate through the staff and his body fell. This detail has no parallel in the Biblical account, which records Solomon's death in First Kings 11:43 without description.

Elijah. Known in the Quran as Ilyas, he is mentioned in Surah Al-An'am 6:85 as among the righteous prophets, and his story is told briefly in Surah As-Saffat 37:123-132. The Biblical account of Elijah is extensive, covering First Kings 17 through Second Kings 2. The Quran confirms him as a messenger sent to a people who worshipped Baal — a detail that matches First Kings 18:18-19 directly, where Elijah confronts the prophets of Baal at the command of God. The Quran in Surah As-Saffat 37:125 records Elijah challenging his people: do you call upon Baal and leave the best of creators? His people rejected him. First Kings 18:20-40 records the dramatic confrontation on Mount Carmel in which fire from heaven consumed Elijah's altar while the altar of Baal's prophets did not ignite. The Quran does not describe this event but confirms his prophethood and records that he was honored and that peace is upon him — Surah As-Saffat 37:130. Both scriptures treat Elijah as a lone prophetic voice who stood against an entire court and culture of idol worship, and both confirm that God protected and honored him.

Elisha. He is mentioned in the Quran in Surah Al-An'am 6:86 and Surah Sad 38:48, placed among the prophets chosen and honored by God. The Bible covers his story in Second Kings 2 through 13. Elisha was the successor of Elijah and performed numerous miracles recorded in the Biblical account, including healing a man of leprosy in Second Kings 5, raising a child from the dead in Second Kings 4:32-35, and multiplying food for a hundred men in Second Kings 4:42-44. The Quran does not describe any of these miracles individually but confirms his prophethood and places him in the same chain as Elijah, Job, and Jonah. His mention, though brief, is significant — it confirms that the Quran views the full succession of Hebrew prophets as part of a single continuous divine mission, without restricting that mission only to the figures who receive extended Quranic narratives.

Jonah. His story is told in the four-chapter Biblical book that bears his name and is referenced across several Quranic passages, including Surah Al-Anbiya 21:87-88, Surah As-Saffat 37:139-148, Surah Al-Qalam 68:48-50, and Surah Yunus 10:98, with an entire chapter named after him — Surah Yunus, though it addresses him only indirectly. Both accounts begin with Jonah leaving his people without permission or before completing his mission. Jonah 1:3 records him fleeing to Tarshish from the presence of God. The Quran in Surah As-Saffat 37:140 says he ran to the laden ship. Both accounts describe lots being cast on the ship, Jonah being identified, and being thrown into the sea. Jonah 1:17 records a great fish swallowing him for three days and nights. The Quran in Surah Al-Anbiya 21:87 records him calling out from within the darknesses — interpreted as the darkness of the fish, the darkness of the sea, and the darkness of night — with the prayer: there is no god except You, glory be to You, indeed I have been among the wrongdoers. Jonah 2:1-9 records a nearly parallel prayer from inside the fish. He is released. Both accounts describe him being cast on a shore, weak and exhausted. Jonah 4:6 records God causing a plant to grow over him for shade. The Quran in Surah As-Saffat 37:146 records the same — a plant of gourd grown to shade him. The accounts differ on the outcome with his people. Jonah 3:4-10 records him preaching to Nineveh, the people repenting, and God relenting from the planned punishment. The Quran in Surah Yunus 10:98 confirms this — describing it as the one town whose people believed in time and whose belief benefited them. Both scriptures use Jonah's story to teach that no one, not even a prophet, is exempt from accountability, and that sincere repentance is always heard.

Zechariah. He is described in both scriptures as a priest and temple servant who received the news of a miraculous son in his old age. Luke 1:5-7 introduces him as a priest in the division of Abijah, righteous before God, with a wife named Elizabeth, both advanced in years and without children. The Quran introduces Zechariah — called Zakariyya — in the context of the temple in Surah Al-Imran 3:37-41 and Surah Maryam 19:2-11. The Quran describes Zakariyya as the guardian of Mary in the sanctuary. He noticed that whenever he entered her chamber, she had provisions already there. He asked her where the food came from, and she said it was from God — Surah Al-Imran 3:37. In that moment, he made a private prayer asking for a righteous child despite his old age and his wife's barrenness — Surah Al-Imran 3:38. Luke 1:11-13 records an angel appearing to Zechariah while he was offering incense in the temple, telling him that his prayer had been heard and that his wife Elizabeth would bear him a son. Both accounts record Zechariah asking for a sign. In Luke 1:18-20, he asks how he can know this, and the angel tells him he will be unable to speak until the birth occurs. The Quran in Surah Maryam 19:10-11 records the same — he asked for a sign and was told he would not speak to people for three days except by gesture. Both accounts describe him emerging from the sanctuary and communicating through signs rather than speech. Both treat this moment as one of the most tender intersections of human longing and divine response in all of sacred history.

John the Baptist. He is called Yahya in the Quran and is confirmed as the son of Zechariah, born in fulfillment of the angelic announcement. Luke 1:57-66 records his birth and the community's amazement. Zechariah's tongue is loosened and he speaks in praise of God — Luke 1:67-79. The Quran in Surah Maryam 19:12-15 describes God calling to Yahya: take the scripture with strength. Yahya was given wisdom while still a child, compassion and purity from God, and was dutiful to his parents, neither arrogant nor rebellious. The Quran records a greeting of peace upon him on three occasions — the day he was born, the day he will die, and the day he will be raised alive — Surah Maryam 19:15. Luke 1:80 says John grew and became strong in spirit. Luke 3:2-3 records him preaching in the wilderness, calling people to repentance and baptizing them. The Quran does not describe his ministry in the wilderness or his death at the hands of Herod — those details are found in Matthew 14:1-12 — but both scriptures confirm him as a prophet honored by God from birth, righteous in conduct, and positioned as a forerunner within the same prophetic mission that would culminate in Jesus.

Mary. She is the only woman referred to by name in the entire Quran, appearing thirty-four times, and Surah Maryam — chapter 19 — is named after her. The Quran and the New Testament both confirm the virgin birth and her elevated status, though they describe her differently. Luke 1:26-35 records the angel Gabriel appearing to Mary, announcing that she would conceive by the Holy Spirit, and Mary accepting the news with faithful submission. The Quran in Surah Maryam 19:16-21 describes Mary withdrawing to a secluded place to the east, a being appearing to her in human form, her expressing fear and asking for God's protection, the being identifying himself as a messenger sent to give her a pure son, and Mary asking how this could happen when no man had touched her. The response in 19:21 is that God simply wills it. Both accounts agree on the miracle, the messenger, and the virginal conception. The Quran then describes Mary going into labor alone under a palm tree in Surah Maryam 19:23-26, the distress she felt, and God calling to her through a voice below, telling her to eat from the dates, drink from the stream, and to rest. When she returned to her people with the infant, they accused her. She was told to point to the child, who would speak — Surah Maryam 19:29. The Quran in Surah Al-Imran 3:42 records God saying to Mary that He had chosen her and purified her and chosen her above the women of all the worlds — a statement of honor that is among the highest conferred on any human being in the Quran. The New Testament in Luke 1:42 records Elizabeth calling Mary blessed among women. Both scriptures are in agreement that Mary was chosen, pure, and uniquely favored.

Jesus. He is described in the Quran as a prophet, a messenger, a word from God cast to Mary, and a spirit from Him — Surah An-Nisa 4:171. An entire chapter of the Quran is named after his mother, and he himself is among the most discussed figures in the text. Both scriptures agree on the virgin birth, that he was sent to the Israelites, and that he performed miracles by God's permission. The Quran in Surah Al-Imran 3:49 records Jesus saying he would create the form of a bird from clay and breathe into it and it would become a bird by God's permission — a detail not found in the canonical Gospels but present in some early Christian texts. He healed those born blind, healed the leper, and raised the dead — all confirmed in both the Quran in Surah Al-Imran 3:49 and in the Gospels, including John 11:43-44 on the raising of Lazarus and Mark 10:51-52 on the healing of a blind man. The Quran in Surah Maryam 19:30-33 records Jesus speaking as an infant in the cradle, declaring himself a servant of God, a prophet, and that peace was upon him the day he was born, the day he would die, and the day he would be raised alive — a passage that parallels the greeting given to John the Baptist in Surah Maryam 19:15. The Quran confirms that Jesus was given the Injil — the Gospel — as a revealed scripture, Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:46. His disciples, called the Hawariyyun in the Quran, are mentioned in Surah Al-Imran 3:52 as those who answered his call and declared themselves those who submit to God. The Quran in Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:112-115 records the disciples asking Jesus to request a table spread from heaven as a sign, and Jesus praying for it. The point where the Quran and the New Testament most significantly diverge is the crucifixion. Surah An-Nisa 4:157-158 states that those who claimed to have killed Jesus did not kill him and did not crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them, and that God raised him up to Himself. The Gospels — Matthew 27, Mark 15, Luke 23, and John 19 — record the crucifixion, death, and resurrection of Jesus as central to their account. Both scriptures anticipate a future role for Jesus: the New Testament through his promised return in Acts 1:11, and the Quran in Surah Az-Zukhruf 43:61, which states that he is a sign for the Hour. Both traditions place him at the center of events that have not yet concluded.
