Adam. He is the first human being in both the Bible and the Quran, created by God from clay and given life through divine breath. The Quran tells his story across multiple chapters, describing how God commanded the angels to bow before Adam as a sign of honor, and how one being — Iblis — refused out of pride. Adam and his wife were placed in a garden and warned away from a single tree. They were deceived, ate from it, and were sent to live on earth. Unlike the Biblical version, the Quran does not name the fruit, does not blame the woman alone, and frames the event less as a fall from grace and more as a temporary lapse that was forgiven. Adam repents, God accepts his repentance, and he is even described as a prophet. The story sets up the central Quranic theme of human weakness, divine mercy, and the ongoing struggle between humanity and Iblis.

Cain and Abel. The Quran tells the story of two sons of Adam who each offered a sacrifice to God, and only one was accepted. The one whose offering was rejected grew furious and threatened to kill his brother. The brother responded with remarkable calm, saying he would not raise his hand against him even to defend himself. The killing happened, and afterward the murderer did not know what to do with the body. God sent a raven that scratched the ground, and by watching it, he learned to bury his brother. The Quran uses this story to deliver one of its most quoted lines — that killing one person is like killing all of humanity, and saving one life is like saving all of humanity. The names Cain and Abel are not used in the Quran; the two are simply referred to as the sons of Adam.

Noah. He appears in the Quran more than almost any other prophet, with an entire chapter named after him. The story follows the same basic arc as Genesis — Noah receives a divine message, spends a long time calling his people to abandon their idols, is largely ignored and mocked, and is eventually commanded to build an ark. The Quran adds a deeply personal tragedy absent from the Biblical account: one of Noah's own sons refuses to board the ark. Noah calls to him as the flood rises, and the son says he will climb a mountain to stay safe. He drowns. Noah cries out to God asking why his son was not saved as part of his family, and God answers that the son was not righteous, and therefore not truly of his household. It is a striking moment, and it places the emotional weight of the story not on the flood itself but on what faith and family really mean.

Hud. He is a prophet found in the Quran but not in the Bible, though he is placed within the same ancient world as the other early prophets and his story immediately follows Noah's in Quranic structure. Hud was sent to a people called Aad, described as powerful builders who lived in a fertile land. They rejected him. A severe wind — described as lasting seven nights and eight days — destroyed them entirely. The ruins of their civilization are referenced as a warning sign, something people could still visit and reflect on. Hud himself survived. His story represents a pattern the Quran repeats throughout: a messenger comes, the people reject him, and a natural disaster follows. He is included here because the Quran presents his story in direct sequence with the Biblical prophets, as part of the same ancient prophetic line.

Salih. Like Hud, Salih is a prophet specific to the Quran with no direct Biblical counterpart, but he is woven into the same narrative thread that connects the Quranic and Biblical worlds. He was sent to a people called Thamud, who carved their homes into mountains. God gave them a she-camel as a sign and commanded them not to harm her. They killed the camel anyway. Three days later, a thunderous blast destroyed the entire civilization. Archaeological sites in northwestern Arabia, particularly at Mada'in Salih, are traditionally associated with the Thamud. The Quran treats Salih and Hud as part of the same unbroken chain that includes Noah, Abraham, and Moses, connecting pre-Islamic Arabian tradition to the broader Biblical prophetic world.

Abraham. He stands at the center of both scriptures, but the Quran gives him a life and personality that goes well beyond Genesis in certain ways. The Quranic Abraham grows up in a community of idol worshippers, including his own father, and from a young age he questions the logic of worshipping statues that can neither hear nor respond. He smashes the idols in the temple and is put on trial. His people try to burn him alive, but the Quran says God commanded the fire to be cool and safe, and Abraham walked out unharmed. He argues with a king about the nature of God in a debate rendered with sharp clarity. He travels, settles in different lands, and is described as a man who submitted completely — the Arabic word muslim, meaning one who submits, is applied directly to him. The Quran calls him Khalilullah, the intimate friend of God, a title that elevates him above nearly all other figures.

The sacrifice of Abraham's son. The Quran tells this story but with one significant difference from Genesis — it never names which son was to be sacrificed. Genesis names Isaac. Islamic tradition has long held that it was Ishmael, the older son, born of Hagar. In the Quranic version, Abraham sees a vision in which he is sacrificing his son. He tells the boy about it, and the son — calmly and willingly — says to do what he has been commanded. As Abraham prepares to carry it out, God intervenes, declares the test fulfilled, and provides a ram in the son's place. The story is deeply tied to the annual festival of Eid al-Adha, observed by over a billion Muslims worldwide, making it one of the most lived and commemorated stories shared between the two traditions.

Lot. He appears in the Quran alongside Abraham, described as a companion and fellow traveler in the prophetic mission. The Quran tells the story of Lot being sent to a city whose people committed what the text describes as a transgression no one had ever committed before — generally understood as referring to the same conduct described in Genesis regarding Sodom. Angels arrived in the form of men, Lot received them as guests, the townspeople gathered outside demanding access to them, and Lot was unable to protect them. The angels revealed their true nature, told Lot to leave with his family before dawn, and warned him not to look back. His wife, who had sided with the people, looked back and was destroyed along with the city. The Quran treats Lot's story as a moral lesson about justice and the protection of guests, a value held in the highest regard in ancient Near Eastern cultures.

Isaac. He is mentioned in the Quran several times, mostly in lists of prophets honored by God, but the Quran gives him less narrative attention than Abraham or Jacob. He is described as a gift given to Abraham in his old age, a righteous prophet, and the father of Jacob. Some Quranic verses describe the angels announcing Isaac's coming birth to Abraham and his elderly wife, who expresses amazement. The Quran confirms him as a prophet and blesses him, placing him in the direct prophetic line that runs from Abraham through Jacob and eventually to Moses. His role in the Quran is more confirmatory than narrative — he validates the lineage rather than carrying a dramatic story of his own.

Jacob. Known in the Quran as Yaqub, he appears most prominently in connection with Joseph, but also in his own right as a prophet and patriarch. He is depicted as a man of deep faith and extraordinary patience. When Joseph disappears and Jacob is left not knowing whether his son is alive or dead, he grieves so deeply that he loses his sight from weeping — yet he never loses his trust in God. The Quran shows him in old age, still believing Joseph will be found, telling his sons to keep searching and never to despair of God's mercy. He is eventually reunited with Joseph, his sight is restored, and the family prostrates before Joseph in a fulfillment of the dream Joseph had as a child. Jacob's character in the Quran is defined by patient faith held under the most painful of circumstances.

Joseph. His story fills an entire chapter of the Quran — Surah Yusuf — and it is described within the text itself as the most beautiful of stories. The core plot follows Genesis closely: Joseph is the favored son, his brothers grow jealous, they throw him into a well and tell their father he was killed by a wolf, he is sold into slavery in Egypt, he rises to a position of trust in a wealthy household, he is falsely accused by the master's wife when he refuses her advances, he is imprisoned, he interprets dreams, and he eventually rises to become a powerful official in Egypt. When famine strikes, his brothers come to Egypt seeking food and do not recognize him. He eventually reveals himself, forgives them, and reunites with his father. The Quran's version is more continuous and emotionally layered than Genesis, and it develops the inner life of Joseph in a way the Biblical account does not always do. The wife of his master, unnamed in the Quran but known in later tradition as Zulaikha, is treated with a degree of complexity — her confession of guilt is included, and the story avoids reducing her to a simple villain.

Job. Known in the Quran as Ayyub, his story is told more briefly than in the Biblical book, but the core is the same. Job was a man blessed with health, wealth, and family who was then struck by severe suffering. The Quran does not describe a debate between God and Satan as the Book of Job does, but it presents a man afflicted for a long period who never abandons his faith. He calls out to God saying that distress has touched him and that God is the most merciful of those who show mercy. God responds, restores his health, and gives him back his family. The Quran uses Job as one of the clearest examples of sabr — patient endurance — a quality placed at the heart of what it means to live with faith. His name has become synonymous in Arabic and Islamic culture with the ability to bear hardship without bitterness.

Shu'ayb. He is a prophet the Quran identifies as having been sent to the people of Midian, the same region where Moses later takes refuge and where Moses' father-in-law lives in the Biblical account. Whether Shu'ayb is the same figure as Jethro, Moses' father-in-law in Exodus, is debated among scholars. The Quran's Shu'ayb condemns his people for dishonest trade — giving short measure, cheating in weights and transactions — and calls them to economic justice. They reject him. An earthquake and a thunderous cry destroy the city. His story sits at the intersection of the Quranic and Biblical worlds, located in a place and time that overlaps directly with the early life of Moses.

Moses. No prophet appears in the Quran more times than Moses. His story stretches across dozens of chapters and covers nearly every major episode from Exodus and beyond — his birth, the basket on the river, his adoption by Pharaoh's household, the killing of an Egyptian, his flight to Midian, the burning bush, the staff that becomes a serpent, the plagues of Egypt, the parting of the sea, the drowning of Pharaoh's army, the giving of the Torah on the mountain, the golden calf, the wandering in the desert. The Quran treats all of this as factual sacred history. What changes between the Biblical and Quranic versions is emphasis. The Quran returns to Moses again and again because his struggle against Pharaoh represents the archetypal conflict between truth and tyranny. Pharaoh in the Quran is not just a historical villain — he is a symbol of any ruler who claims divine status and crushes those beneath him. Moses' stutter, his self-doubt, his asking God to let his brother Aaron support him — these human details appear in the Quran and give him a vulnerability that makes him one of the most fully realized figures in the entire text.

The crossing of the sea. This episode receives its own weight in the Quran beyond just being part of Moses' story. The Quran describes the moment the sea was struck and it split, the Israelites crossed, and Pharaoh's army followed and was swallowed. Then the Quran adds something the Book of Exodus does not — it says God preserved Pharaoh's body so that future generations could see him as a sign. This detail has fascinated historians and theologians for centuries. Some have pointed to the discovery of mummified pharaohs as an unintentional fulfillment of this statement, though no historical identification has been conclusively established. The Quran presents the drowning not just as liberation but as a lesson meant to outlast the event itself.

The Israelites in the wilderness. After the crossing, the Quran follows the Israelites through their time in the desert in ways that mirror Numbers and Deuteronomy. They are given manna and quail. They complain about food. They are commanded to enter a holy land and refuse out of fear, saying they will not enter while oppressors dwell there. Moses is told they will wander for forty years as a consequence. They worship the golden calf while Moses is on the mountain receiving the Torah — and Aaron, who tried to stop them, is confronted by a furious Moses on his return. The Quran also tells the story of a cow the Israelites were commanded to slaughter as part of a murder investigation, a story unique in its detail. A man was killed, the killer was unknown, and God commanded them to sacrifice a specific cow and strike the dead man with part of it — at which point he revived briefly, named his killer, and died again. This story gives Surah Al-Baqarah, the longest chapter of the Quran, its name: The Cow.

Elijah. Known in the Quran as Ilyas, he is mentioned briefly but clearly identified as a messenger sent to a people who worshipped Baal — directly matching the Biblical account in First Kings. The Quran's treatment is short, confirming his prophethood and recording that his people rejected him before being destroyed, while Elijah himself was honored. He is praised with a greeting that echoes through the text. The Quran does not describe the contest on Mount Carmel, the fire from heaven, or his being carried away in a chariot — but it affirms his place in the prophetic line and his message as genuine. His mention is brief enough that many readers of the Quran miss the direct connection to one of the most dramatic figures in the Hebrew Bible.

Elisha. He appears in the Quran in a single verse alongside other prophets listed as those God chose and favored above the nations. He is called Al-Yasa in Arabic, and beyond his inclusion in the list of honored prophets, the Quran adds almost nothing about his specific story. The connection to the Biblical Elisha — successor to Elijah — is clear through the name and the prophetic context, but the Quran does not recount his miracles or his relationship with Elijah. His presence in the list confirms that the Quran considers the full succession of Hebrew prophets as part of one unbroken divine mission.

Jonah. The Quran tells the story of Jonah, called Yunus, in a way that is recognizable from the Book of Jonah but also distinctive. Jonah left his people without waiting for God's permission, boarded a ship, the ship was in distress, lots were drawn and Jonah was thrown into the sea, and he was swallowed by a great fish. Inside the fish, he called out in darkness — the Quran preserves his prayer: that there is no god but God, and that he had been among the wrongdoers. He was released onto a shore, weak and ill, and a tree grew over him for shade. The Quran then says his people — unlike in the Biblical account where they are Nineveh — believed and were spared destruction. Jonah is one of the few prophets the Quran treats as having made a genuine error in judgment, and his story becomes a meditation on repentance and the right way to carry a prophetic mission.

David. Known in the Quran as Dawud, he appears in connection with Goliath — called Jalut — and later as a king and prophet. The battle between the Israelites and Goliath's forces is recounted, with David killing Goliath and the Israelites routing the army. The Quran describes God giving David sovereignty and wisdom and teaching him what He willed. David is described as someone who could understand the speech of birds, who had the mountains and birds join him in praise, and who was given the craft of making armor from iron. He is also given the Zabur — the Psalms — as a revealed scripture. The Quran also references a story of two disputants who came to David: one had ninety-nine ewes and wanted the other's single ewe. David judged quickly in the rich man's favor, then realized he had been hasty. Scholars and commentators have long read this as a veiled reference to David's behavior with Bathsheba, though the Quran does not name her or describe that event explicitly.

Solomon. He receives more narrative attention in the Quran than almost any other king-prophet. The Quran describes Solomon as having been given command over the wind, over jinn who built monuments and dived for pearls, and over the speech of birds and animals. One of the most extended Quranic stories involves the hoopoe bird, which brings Solomon news of a queen in the south who rules over a great kingdom and worships the sun. Solomon sends a letter to the Queen of Sheba — called Bilqis in later tradition — inviting her to submit. She arrives, and before she reaches him, Solomon has one of the jinn transport her throne to his court in the blink of an eye. She is then invited into a palace with a floor made of glass so clear she mistakes it for water and lifts her robe before stepping in. She recognizes the truth and submits to God. The Quran also mentions a trial in which Solomon nearly cursed the wind, the jinn, and his own power when his horse distracted him from prayer, and he repented. His death is described with dry humor — he died leaning on his staff, and the jinn continued working thinking he was alive, until a worm had eaten through the staff and his body fell.

Zechariah. The Quran tells the story of Zechariah — called Zakariyya — as the guardian of Mary in the temple. Every time he entered her chamber, he found food already there. He asked her where it came from, and she said it was from God. In that moment, Zechariah prayed for a child of his own, even though he was very old and his wife was barren. The angels answered that he would have a son named John — Yahya in Arabic. Zechariah asked for a sign, and was told he would not be able to speak for three days except through gesture. The story is told with quiet emotional depth, a man who has spent his life in the service of the temple asking for something he hardly dares to hope for, and receiving it.

John the Baptist. Called Yahya in the Quran, he is confirmed as a prophet born to Zechariah in answer to that prayer. The Quran describes him as someone given wisdom while still a child, full of compassion and purity, dutiful to his parents, and not arrogant or rebellious. He is greeted with peace on three occasions mentioned in the same verse — the day he was born, the day he will die, and the day he will be raised alive again. The Quran does not describe his role baptizing people, his preaching in the wilderness, or his death at the hands of Herod — those details are absent. What the Quran offers instead is a portrait of a soul: pure, gentle, and wise from the very beginning of his life.

Mary. She is the only woman referred to by name in the entire Quran, and an entire chapter is named after her. The Quran tells of her birth, her dedication to the temple by her mother, her upbringing under Zechariah's care, and a moment when an angel appears to her in the form of a man. She fears him and asks for protection. He tells her he is a messenger from God and that she will conceive a pure son. She asks how this is possible when no man has touched her. He says God simply wills it so. The Quran describes her going into seclusion, giving birth alone under a palm tree, being told to shake the tree for dates and drink from a stream flowing beneath her, and then being told not to speak when she returns — because if anyone questions her, she is to point to the child, who will speak for himself. The Quran treats Mary with a gravity and honor that many readers are surprised to find. She is described as the best of all women, chosen and purified above all other women of the world.

Jesus. Called Isa in the Quran, he is one of the most discussed figures in the text. The Quran affirms the virgin birth, his miracles, and his role as a messenger. He spoke as an infant in the cradle to defend his mother. He fashioned birds from clay and breathed life into them. He healed the blind and the leper. He raised the dead. He was given the Injil — the Gospel — as a revealed scripture. His disciples, called the Hawariyyun, are mentioned as those who answered his call and declared themselves as Muslims — those who submit. The Quran also recounts the story of the disciples asking Jesus to ask God for a table spread from heaven, and Jesus praying for it as a sign and a blessing. Where the Quran departs most sharply from the New Testament is on the crucifixion. The Quran states that Jesus was not killed and was not crucified — that it was made to appear so — and that God raised him to Himself. He is described as a sign, a mercy, and a word from God, but the text is firm that he was a human messenger and not divine. The Quran anticipates his return before the end of time, placing him at the center of the final chapter of human history.
